Albert Einstein is often (probably apocryphally) credited with calling compound interest "the
eighth wonder of the world." Whether or not he actually said it, the underlying math explains why
the phrase stuck.

## Simple interest vs. compound interest

**Simple interest** is calculated only on your original principal, every period:

```
Interest = P × r × t
```

**Compound interest** is calculated on your principal *plus* all previously earned interest:

```
A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)
```

Where `P` is principal, `r` is the annual interest rate, `n` is how many times per year interest
compounds, and `t` is time in years. The difference between these two formulas seems small over
a year or two - but it compounds, quite literally, over decades.

## A concrete comparison

Take $10,000 at 7% annual interest over 30 years:

| Method | Formula | Result after 30 years |
|---|---|---|
| Simple interest | P × r × t, added to P | $31,000 |
| Compound interest (annual) | P(1+r)^t | ≈ $76,123 |

That's a difference of over $45,000 on the same starting amount and the same rate, purely because
compound interest lets earlier interest start earning interest of its own.

## Why compounding frequency matters less than people think

You might expect daily compounding to dramatically outperform monthly or annual compounding, but
the actual difference is usually small - often well under 1% of the final balance for typical
consumer rates. What matters far more is:

1. **The interest rate** - even a couple of percentage points compounds into a large difference
   over decades.
2. **Time** - the earlier money starts compounding, the more periods it has to benefit from
   growth-on-growth.
3. **Consistent contributions** - adding money regularly, even in small amounts, meaningfully
   boosts the final balance since each contribution starts compounding immediately.
